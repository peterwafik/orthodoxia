Bible study plans for every kind of reader

For a young person at home, reading alone, who doesn't catch everything the first time and needs a reason to stay interested. This page shows the plans I'd offer, how a plan adapts to the reader's personality, three ways the plan screens could look, and three ways to create the plans. Choose at the bottom, then copy your choices back to me.

1 · Plans by interest

Eight ways in

Each plan starts from something the reader already cares about and uses a part of the site that exists today. Solid chips are pages already built. Dashed chips are things still to build.

2 · Plans by personality

Same passage, wrapped for the reader

Personality changes how a day is served, not what is read. Every day has the same core: a hook, the passage, a short explanation, and one question. The reader's style adds one extra piece, and their pace sets how long the passage is (5, 15 or 30 minutes).

I'd use four plain reading styles rather than personality-test labels like MBTI. Readers recognise themselves in them straight away. Pick one to see Day 1 of Meet Jesus change.

Meet Jesus · Day 1 of 16≈ 5 min
Hook

Mark writes fast. In twenty verses: a wild prophet in the desert, a voice from heaven, forty days of testing, and four fishermen who drop everything. And Mark is the founder of our Church in Alexandria.

Read

Mark 1:1–20: opens in the Bible reader, with commentary one tap away.

Understand

“Gospel” means good news. In Mark's day the word announced a king's victory. John the Baptist goes ahead like a herald, getting the road ready for the King.

Think

Simon and Andrew left their nets “at once”. What would be hard for you to leave?

B · Daily cards

Card 1 of 5 · HookMark writes fast. In twenty verses: a wild prophet, a voice from heaven, and four fishermen who drop everything.

A day is 4–6 cards you swipe or tap through: Hook → Read → Understand → Think → (your style's extra) → Done. A small strip at the top shows the plan's progress.

  • Bite-sized: one idea per screen, made for short attention
  • Feels like the apps this age group already uses
  • A clear “done” moment every day
  • The longer passage still opens in the full reader

4 · How the plans get made

Three ways to create the plans

This matters most for quality. A plan is only as good as its hooks and explanations, and those need writing.

1 · Written by hand, fixed

Each plan is written day by day by a person and reviewed by a priest. Everyone on the same plan reads the same days.

  • Best quality and a single voice
  • Easy for a priest to approve as a whole
  • Slow: eight plans of about 30 days is around 240 days to write
  • Doesn't adapt to the reader

2 · Assembled from the site's data

Rules pick the passages from what the site already holds: people from the Bible history chart, places from the atlas, questions from Faith & Evidence, psalms from the Agpeya. The explanation is taken from the commentary.

  • Fast: many plans at once
  • Always in step with the rest of the site
  • No hooks, so it reads like a list of references
  • The commentary is long and scholarly for this reader
My pick

3 · Building blocks and a short quiz

Write about 150 reusable “days” once. Each has a passage, hook, short explanation and question, tagged by interest, length and difficulty. A four-question quiz finds the reader's interest, pace, style and experience, then builds their plan from the blocks. Progress is kept on the reader's device, so there are no accounts.

  • Every reader gets a plan that fits, from one set of writing
  • A block can serve several plans (Joseph is in “Big Story” and “People”)
  • Each block is small enough for a priest to review
  • More to build than option 1

With option 3, I'd draft the blocks from the existing commentary, people, places and evidence data. Each would be marked Draft, as in the Holy Mass, until a person has reviewed it. Bible text is never rewritten. Only the short framing around it is written new, in Arabic and English.
